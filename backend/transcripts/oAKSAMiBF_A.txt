Turns out the massive drop hammer isn't just hitting the hot rod at random. It's using a mold to sequentially add features and shape the metal. Each hit or impact, forms the circular rod a little bit more until the final rough form of the pliers emerges.

The heat makes the metal malleable, and all of the forming has to be done quickly before the metal has a chance to cool down and harden once more.

Fun fact, the ground beneath these hammers gets so pounded into oblivion that Channel Lock has to regularly rebuild the foundation under each of the hammers. It's a beast. But when you make millions of tools every year, you got to do what you got to do.

The unfortunate problem with stamping and forging parts is now the material has work hardened and is more difficult to shape and machine, which means that Channel Lock has to put the entire bin of metal parts back into a furnace to anneal the metal. This reduces the internal stresses of the metal and makes the parts easier to machine. Since that's where we're headed next, not only do we have to punch out the channel of the Channel Lock pliers, but we have to mill out the pivot points and machine the tongue and groove portion. You know, the part where you can adjust the opening of the pliers to grip items of different sizes.

I've never seen so many in the same place at the same time. That's so cool.

The sides are then mated up with each other and sent through a belt sander, and lasered one last time with the Channel Lock logo. A large rivet is then pressed into place permanently, joining both halves together forever.
